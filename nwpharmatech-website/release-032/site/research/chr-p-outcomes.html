Clinical high risk for psychosis: what happens next?

Some people experience early, milder or brief psychotic-like symptoms and seek help. Specialist services can assess them against established criteria for "clinical high risk for psychosis" (CHR-P). This page summarises what published research says about what happens next, and where the uncertainty lies.

Not one path

  • Transition. In a pooled meta-analysis, about 1 in 4 people who met CHR-P criteria developed a psychotic disorder within three years. Most did not. (Salazar de Pablo et al., 2021)
  • Remission. A 2026 meta-analysis of 51 studies (about 10,000 people) found that around a third of people at clinical high risk went into remission. (npj Schizophrenia, 2026)
  • These figures come from different studies with different definitions and follow-up periods, so they shouldn't be added together.

What current guidelines recommend

UK guidelines recommend psychological interventions, such as cognitive behavioural therapy, for people at clinical high risk. They advise against offering antipsychotic medicines to reduce the risk of psychosis. No medicine is approved for this group. (NICE CG155 and CG178)

Why this matters for research

Predicting who will follow which path is still one of psychiatry's hardest open problems. Careful early-intervention research needs better ways to stratify people, outcomes that go beyond transition alone, and transparency about what isn't yet known.
