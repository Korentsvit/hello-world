The clinical need

People at clinical high risk of psychosis

Some young people seek help for distressing experiences that resemble mild or brief symptoms of psychosis. Specialist services can identify them as being at "clinical high risk". Many are struggling now, and some go on to develop a psychotic disorder. There is no licensed medicine for this group.

What "clinical high risk" means

Clinical high risk of psychosis (CHR-P, sometimes called an "at-risk mental state" or "ultra-high risk") describes people, usually adolescents and young adults, who have sought help and who meet defined criteria on a structured clinical assessment. These criteria typically include one or more of:

  • Attenuated psychotic symptoms: unusual thoughts, perceptions or suspiciousness that are milder, briefer or held with less conviction than in psychosis.
  • Brief limited intermittent psychotic symptoms: short episodes of psychotic symptoms that resolve on their own.
  • Genetic risk with functional decline: a close relative with a psychotic disorder, together with a recent drop in day-to-day functioning.

Assessments such as the Comprehensive Assessment of At-Risk Mental States (CAARMS) and the Structured Interview for Psychosis-Risk Syndromes (SIPS) are widely used to make this identification.

  • Established

    Criteria for clinical high risk of psychosis identify a group at substantially higher risk of developing a psychotic disorder than the general population.

    Fusar-Poli et al., 2013, JAMA Psychiatry. Reference
  • Established

    Most people who meet these criteria do not go on to develop psychosis. Pooled estimates suggest roughly one in four do so within three years, with considerable variation between services and studies.

    Salazar de Pablo et al., 2021, JAMA Psychiatry; Fusar-Poli et al., 2012, Archives of General Psychiatry. References
  • Established

    Many people who do not develop psychosis still experience ongoing symptoms, anxiety or depression, and difficulties with work, study and relationships.

    Addington et al., 2011, American Journal of Psychiatry; Fusar-Poli et al., 2013. References

A note on language

Being at "clinical high risk" is not a diagnosis of psychosis, and it does not mean psychosis is inevitable. Most people in this group do not develop a psychotic disorder.

We use the term because it is standard in research and clinical services, and because it identifies people who deserve care and better options now.

What care is available today

Psychological therapy

In England, NICE guidance recommends offering individual cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT), with or without family intervention, to people considered at increased risk of psychosis.

Established

Monitoring & support

Specialist early intervention services offer assessment, monitoring and treatment of co-occurring problems such as anxiety, depression and substance use.

Established

No licensed medicine

No medicine is licensed to reduce symptoms or prevent psychosis in this group. NICE advises against offering antipsychotic medication to people at risk to decrease the risk of, or prevent, psychosis.

Established

Source: NICE clinical guideline CG178, Psychosis and schizophrenia in adults: prevention and management (2014, updated). Reference. Guidance differs between countries.

Why new options are being investigated

Psychological approaches help many people, but not everyone can access them, and not everyone benefits. Antipsychotic medicines carry side effects that are generally considered unjustified in people who do not have psychosis and most of whom will not develop it. Researchers have therefore looked for interventions that might be both helpful and well tolerated in this group.

Cannabidiol (CBD) is one of the candidates being investigated. The science page explains why, and is clear about the limits of what is currently known.

  • Open question

    Whether any medicine, including cannabidiol, can safely reduce symptoms, improve functioning or lower the risk of psychosis in people at clinical high risk.

If you are worried about yourself or someone else

Please talk to your GP or a mental health professional. In the UK, NHS early intervention in psychosis services can assess young people who may be at risk. If you are in crisis, call 999 or NHS 111 (option 2), or Samaritans on 116 123. Outside the UK, contact your local health or emergency services.
