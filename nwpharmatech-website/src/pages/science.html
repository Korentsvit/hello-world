The science

Cannabidiol and psychosis: what we know, and what we don't

Cannabidiol (CBD) is a compound found in the cannabis plant. Unlike THC, it does not produce a "high". Early clinical studies have raised the possibility that it could be relevant to psychosis, but the evidence is limited, and it has not been shown to prevent psychosis or treat people at clinical high risk.

About cannabidiol

  • Established

    CBD is not intoxicating and has a different pharmacological profile from delta-9-tetrahydrocannabinol (THC), the main psychoactive component of cannabis.

  • Established

    A purified, pharmaceutical-grade CBD medicine is approved in the UK, EU and US for certain rare, severe forms of epilepsy. Its safety has been studied in controlled trials in that context.

    Devinsky et al., 2017, New England Journal of Medicine. Reference
  • Established

    CBD can cause side effects, including sleepiness, reduced appetite, diarrhoea and changes in liver blood tests, and it can interact with other medicines. This is one reason it must be studied carefully, under medical supervision.

    Prescribing information for licensed CBD; Devinsky et al., 2017.
  • Established

    Consumer CBD products (oils, gummies, vapes) vary widely in content and quality, and are not the same as a pharmaceutical formulation developed and tested under medicines regulation.

CBD and THC are different

Heavy use of high-THC cannabis is associated with an increased risk of psychosis. CBD is a different compound, and some researchers have hypothesised that it may act in some respects in the opposite direction to THC.

That hypothesis is not proven. It is one of the reasons CBD is being studied.

Bhattacharyya et al., 2010, Neuropsychopharmacology. Reference

Emerging evidence

What clinical studies have found so far

A small number of randomised studies have tested CBD in people with established psychosis, and a few smaller studies have examined its effects in people at clinical high risk. Their findings are informative but not conclusive: the studies were small, short and used different doses and formulations, and results have been mixed.

In people with schizophrenia or established psychosis

  • Emerging evidence

    In a 6-week randomised trial of 88 patients, CBD (1,000 mg/day) added to existing antipsychotic treatment was associated with a greater reduction in positive psychotic symptoms than placebo, and was generally well tolerated.

    McGuire et al., 2018, American Journal of Psychiatry. Reference
  • Emerging evidence

    In a 4-week randomised trial of 42 patients with acute schizophrenia, CBD and the antipsychotic amisulpride produced similar symptom improvement, with fewer side effects reported with CBD.

    Leweke et al., 2012, Translational Psychiatry. Reference
  • Emerging evidence

    In a 6-week randomised trial in outpatients with chronic schizophrenia, CBD (600 mg/day) did not improve cognition or symptoms compared with placebo.

    Boggs et al., 2018, Psychopharmacology. Reference

In people at clinical high risk of psychosis

  • Emerging evidence

    In an experimental study, a single dose of CBD partially normalised differences in brain activity (measured with functional MRI during a memory task) in people at clinical high risk, compared with placebo.

    Bhattacharyya et al., 2018, JAMA Psychiatry. Reference
  • Emerging evidence

    In a small study, one week of CBD treatment was associated with a partial reduction in some physiological and psychological responses to a laboratory stress test in people at clinical high risk.

    Appiah-Kusi et al., 2020, Psychopharmacology. Reference

What these studies do not show

Changes in brain imaging or stress responses in an experimental setting are not the same as clinical benefit. None of these studies shows that CBD reduces symptoms, improves day-to-day functioning, or prevents psychosis in people at clinical high risk.

How might CBD work?

The mechanism is not established. Proposed explanations, each supported by preclinical or early clinical data, include:

  • effects on the endocannabinoid system, including levels of the signalling molecule anandamide;
  • modulation of brain activity in regions implicated in psychosis, such as the hippocampus and striatum;
  • effects on stress and anxiety responses, which may contribute to the emergence of psychotic symptoms.

Open question Which, if any, of these mechanisms is clinically relevant.

The questions that remain open

These are the questions that matter most to people at clinical high risk, their families and clinicians. Our planned Phase 2B study is designed to address the first four.

  • Open question

    Does NWPT-SM32300 reduce symptoms in people at clinical high risk of psychosis?

  • Open question

    Is there a dose–response relationship, and which dose, if any, offers the best balance of benefit and tolerability?

  • Open question

    What is its safety profile in this population, which is often young and may be taking other medicines?

  • Open question

    How well is it tolerated, and can people take it consistently over the study period?

  • Open question

    Could it reduce the risk of developing psychosis, or improve longer-term functioning? Answering this would require larger and longer studies.
