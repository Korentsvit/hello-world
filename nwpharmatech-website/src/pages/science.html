The science

Cannabidiol and psychosis: what is known and what is not

Cannabidiol (CBD) is a compound from the cannabis plant that does not produce a "high". A small number of clinical studies suggest it may be relevant to psychosis. Evidence in people at clinical high risk comes from a few small, short studies, and no study has shown that it prevents psychosis.

These studies did not use NWPT-SM32300

Every study summarised on this page tested other cannabidiol preparations, at different doses and in different people. Their results cannot be assumed to apply to NWPT-SM32300. Studies of NWPT-SM32300 itself are described on the programme page.

About cannabidiol

Established

CBD is not intoxicating and acts differently from delta-9-tetrahydrocannabinol (THC), the main psychoactive component of cannabis.

A purified CBD medicine (Epidyolex in the UK and EU; Epidiolex in the US) is licensed for certain rare, severe epilepsies. Its known side effects include sleepiness, diarrhoea, tiredness and changes in liver blood tests, especially alongside the epilepsy medicine valproate, and it can interact with other medicines such as clobazam. That is why CBD must be studied carefully, under medical supervision.

CBD products sold as food supplements or wellness products vary widely in content and quality, and are not tested in the same way as medicines.

Sources: Epidyolex Summary of Product Characteristics; Devinsky et al. 2017. References

CBD and THC

Emerging evidence

Daily use of high-potency (high-THC) cannabis is associated with higher odds of psychotic disorder. In experimental studies, CBD and THC had opposite effects on some measures of brain function.

Whether CBD counteracts processes involved in psychosis is not established. It is one reason CBD is being studied.

Sources: Di Forti et al. 2019; Bhattacharyya et al. 2010. References

Studies in people with schizophrenia

Emerging evidence

Three randomised trials, each small and short, using different CBD preparations and doses. Results are mixed.

CBD added to antipsychotic treatment

Design
Randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled
People
88 adults with schizophrenia
CBD
1,000 mg a day for 6 weeks

Positive psychotic symptoms fell more with CBD than with placebo, and clinicians were more likely to rate patients as improved. CBD was generally well tolerated.

McGuire et al. 2018, American Journal of Psychiatry. Reference

CBD compared with an antipsychotic

Design
Randomised, double-blind, active comparator (amisulpride)
People
42 inpatients with acute schizophrenia
CBD
Up to 800 mg a day for 4 weeks

Symptoms improved to a similar extent in both groups. Fewer side effects were reported with CBD.

Leweke et al. 2012, Translational Psychiatry. Reference

CBD in chronic schizophrenia

Design
Randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled
People
36 outpatients with chronic schizophrenia
CBD
600 mg a day for 6 weeks

CBD did not improve cognition or symptoms compared with placebo.

Boggs et al. 2018, Psychopharmacology. Reference

Studies in people at clinical high risk

Emerging evidence

Three small studies. Two were experiments measuring brain activity and stress responses; one, reported only as a brief letter, measured symptoms over three weeks.

Brain activity after a single dose

Design
Randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled, with a healthy comparison group
People
33 people at clinical high risk; 19 healthy volunteers
CBD
A single 600 mg dose

During a memory task, brain activity in people at clinical high risk who took CBD was intermediate between the placebo group and healthy volunteers in several regions.

Bhattacharyya et al. 2018, JAMA Psychiatry. Reference

Response to a stress test after one week

Design
Randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled, with a healthy comparison group
People
32 people at clinical high risk; 26 healthy volunteers
CBD
600 mg a day for 7 days

Anxiety and stress responses to a public-speaking test were intermediate between the placebo group and healthy volunteers. The cortisol (stress hormone) response did not differ significantly from placebo.

Appiah-Kusi et al. 2020, Psychopharmacology. Reference

Symptoms after three weeks

Design
Randomised, placebo-controlled; published as a short research letter
People
33 people at clinical high risk (16 CBD, 17 placebo)
CBD
600 mg a day for 21 days

After adjusting for starting scores, symptom and distress scores were lower with CBD than with placebo, and CBD was well tolerated. The study was small and short.

Bhattacharyya et al. 2024, World Psychiatry. Reference

What these studies do not show

Changes in brain scans or stress responses are not the same as clinical benefit. One small, three-week study reported lower symptom scores, which has not been confirmed in a larger trial. A larger UK trial (CANTOP) was funded but did not go ahead because the study medicine could not be supplied. No study shows that CBD improves day-to-day functioning or prevents psychosis in people at clinical high risk.

Source: CANTOP-RCT report, NIHR Journals Library. Reference

How might CBD work?

Open question

The mechanism is not established. Explanations under study include effects on the endocannabinoid system (including the signalling molecule anandamide), on activity in brain regions implicated in psychosis such as the hippocampus and striatum, and on stress and anxiety responses. Which, if any, of these matter clinically is unknown.

Questions our research aims to answer

Open question
  • Does NWPT-SM32300 reduce symptoms in people at clinical high risk of psychosis?
  • Is there a dose–response relationship, and which dose, if any, gives the best balance of benefit and tolerability?
  • What is its safety profile in this population, which is often young and may be taking other medicines?
  • Can people take it as intended over the study period?
  • Could it lower the risk of psychosis or improve longer-term functioning? Answering this would need larger, longer studies.
